Just writing a little.

Progesterone hit me hard. Last week, I had no motivation at all.

As a child, I often felt as though there were many brains inside my head. While one brain thought about one thing, another thought about something else, and another about something else again—layers of brains, layers of thoughts. As I grew up, they gradually seemed to become one. I could no longer clearly hear the layer beneath the one on the surface. Perhaps this has something to do with changes in the brain's white and gray matter.

As a child, I rarely looked back on my life. Perhaps each day's new discoveries were already more than enough, and my brain still had plenty of room and capacity to grow. After turning twenty, I began revisiting my life frequently: taking memories apart, picking them up, putting them down, and piecing them back together. This frightens me. Cognitive neuroscience tells us that whenever we retrieve a memory and put it back, we are actually editing and coloring it. Human memory is very prone to error. Every time I recall a fragment, I worry that the next time I remember it, it will be further from what really happened. I also worry that I might forget to put it back this time, and be unable to recall it next time. Or why, among all the things that happened in a particular year of childhood, do I remember only this one? Are the things I haven't recalled already disappearing from my mind? My memories expand, contract, and collapse. I feel as though I lose a little memory every day, a part of myself. Of course, I could also see this as updating, building, and gaining a part of myself every day. Still, forgetting frightens me. I am afraid that one day I will forget something I don't want to forget. Perhaps it is our instinct for loss aversion.

My memory does work every day to resist the arrow of time and the increase of entropy.

As a child, I wanted to invent a conveyor belt: from any corner of the world, anywhere on a street, you could step onto it and be transported somewhere else.

A few days ago, I saw some students' course readings about Islamic culture in the library. They said that Islam means submission. My understanding is that this means submitting oneself to something beyond one's control: something immense, against which one can feel one's own smallness and thereby let go of pain or responsibility. While swimming, I had a sudden realization. Swimming is my submission to the water. The water and I are both lonely. My mind is blank, and at this moment, this stretch of water is mine.

Sport is a way of experiencing life when the body reaches its limits.

I like running. Part of it is, of course, the feeling of release in both body and mind when the endorphins kick in. But what I like even more is how, when my body reaches its limit, every breath becomes extraordinarily clear. One second feels like ten. My brain seems to consciously control and feel every step and every breath. I have never felt so clearly that I am alive, driving my legs forward while my body bears intense pain. Through that pain and suffering, the outline of life becomes vivid and distinct. I always feel the force of life at the edge of “dying.” I have never been so grateful for my legs. In the final kilometer, I want to run as though I will lose them tomorrow.

I think Clawdbot is hype, as it is essentially a wrapper plus API access plus an LLM, and the real power here is still the LLM haha. for the consciousness part, we simply do not know and cannot tell. By the definition provided by the Turing test, I would say they are intelligent. whether they are conscious depends on how consciousness is defined.

When we refer to consciousness, we usually associate consciousness with subjective experience and awareness of the self, the body, and the environment. from a carbon-based mammalian perspective, all LLMs are silicon-based simulations of the computation of intelligence involving language, which is associated with parts of cognition, subjective experience, and awareness, and are designed to be aligned with the subspace of the communication channel that humans deploy. In this sense, it is a simulation of consciousness.

regardless, intelligence does not necessarily require the existence of consciousness. who can truly tell the difference between a simulation of consciousness and consciousness itself though. i myself am conscious and a simulation of consciousness at the same time.

I like the idea you mentioned the other day about new technologies liberating us by perhaps sacrificing some of our brain’s functionalities but allowing us to focus on what may be more important. LLM make some capability less significant, for example, basic coding, image editing, or writing LLM style garbage. At the same time, it makes other abilities more important, such as insight, aesthetic judgment, structural thinking, and decision-making. As someone who works closely with AI, I have never felt that it can replace critical thinking. Most of the comments it generates that feel “novel” or “critical” are ultimately derived from books written by people. However, what I truly like about is if someone is genuinely curious, LLMs can dramatically break down barriers of language and access to information. As long as you want to learn, you can keep asking questions, keep learning, keep discussing, and enjoy something close to free education, which is a huge privilege that was unimaginable for scholars in ancient time.

I believe everyone has an intrinsic curiosity about the world when they are children, and our education system should encourage this kind of self-driven motivation. Too much external reward can undermine internal motivation. Kant once argued that when people believe in God, it becomes difficult to tell whether their goodness is genuine or merely instrumental(done for the reward of going to heaven), thus undermining free will. I think the same applies to the pursuit of knowledge. If someone has a pure desire to learn, LLMs can satisfy and support that curiosity. But if everything is driven by instrumental goals within an evaluation system, then LLMs may actually hinder real learning. But that’s not the fault of LLM, rather, the mindset of ppl who use them this way; or rather, the system that nudge ppl to use them this way.
